Stroke rehab that costs about thirty dollars.

ReMoRehab is a free browser app that guides mirror therapy after a stroke, and an open 3D-printable headset that turns a phone you already own into the display. No clinic visit, no subscription, no specialist hardware.

In Maryland's 1st district, the need is highest where the help is thinnest.

Salisbury sits in Wicomico County, on Maryland's Lower Eastern Shore. The CDC models chronic-disease rates for every county in the country. On the Shore, the numbers run in the wrong direction on both sides of the ledger at once: more stroke, more disability, fewer providers.

  • 3.4%of adults in Wicomico County have had a stroke — against a Maryland county median of 2.9%
  • 4.0%in neighbouring Somerset County, the highest of any county in Maryland
  • 12.9%of Wicomico adults have serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs
  • 1 in 2,500health providers per resident in Caroline County, against 1 in 1,000 in Baltimore County

Somerset County's 4.0% is not far off Arkansas, the worst state in the country at 4.8%, and it sits in one of the wealthiest states in the union. Four of the nine Shore counties are above the Maryland median on every column in that table.

And the providers are not there

Nearly the entire Eastern Shore carries federal designation as a medically underserved area and a health professional shortage area. The five Maryland counties with the fewest primary care physicians per capita are all on the Shore. More than a million Marylanders live in underserved areas, and the largest share of them live in these rural counties.

For someone recovering from a stroke, that arithmetic is brutal. Outpatient therapy means a standing appointment, several times a week, for months — and on the Shore it often means a long drive each way, arranged by someone who can no longer drive themselves.

The same gap, at national scale.

The Lower Shore is not unusual. It is an early, sharp version of a problem the whole country has: stroke is survivable now, and what happens afterwards depends almost entirely on whether you can reach a therapist often enough, for long enough.

  • 795,000strokes in the United States every year — about 610,000 of them a person's first
  • 1 in 4of those strokes happen to someone who has already had one
  • >50%of stroke survivors aged 65 and over are left with reduced mobility
  • $56.2bnin US stroke-related costs across 2019–2020, including missed work

Stroke is a leading cause of serious long-term disability in the United States. The therapy that limits that disability is not exotic — much of it is repetition, done often, early, and kept up. The barrier is almost never that the exercise is complicated. It is that getting to a room with a therapist in it, three times a week for three months, is beyond a lot of households.

So we moved the room into the phone.

ReMoRehab has two halves. Both are free, and neither needs anything you would have to special-order.

The app

A web page. It opens your camera, mirrors your working limb across the centre line so your brain sees two good limbs moving together, and walks you through timed sets. It counts your reps, keeps your streak, and runs offline after the first load. Nothing is uploaded — the video never leaves the device.

The headset

An 11-part 3D-printable shroud that holds the phone at the right distance behind a pair of lenses, so the mirrored view fills your field of vision instead of sitting in your hand. About $3 of filament and a $10 pair of lenses. Every file is in this repository.

How a session works

  1. Pick the side you are working

    Tell the app which arm is the affected one. It mirrors the other.

  2. Put the phone in, or just hold it

    The headset makes the illusion stronger by filling your vision, but the app works perfectly well propped on a table if you have not printed one yet.

  3. Move the good limb, watch the reflection

    The app paces you through sets with a visible timer and counts the reps. You keep your eyes on the mirrored limb the whole time — that is the part that matters.

  4. Come back tomorrow

    Sessions are logged on the device so you and anyone helping you can see what has actually been done, not what was meant to be done.

What the evidence actually says.

Mirror therapy is a real, long-studied intervention, and it is worth being precise about how strong the case is — including where it is weak.

The case for it

The 2018 Cochrane review pooled 36 trials covering 1,173 participants and found mirror therapy improved upper-limb motor function at the end of treatment, with a standardised mean difference of 0.47 (95% CI 0.27 to 0.67), graded as moderate-quality evidence. A similar effect was found on motor impairment across 39 trials: SMD 0.49 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.66).

The case against overclaiming

Those are standardised effect sizes, which do not translate cleanly into points on a clinical scale. Durability is genuinely unproven: only two trials with 88 participants measured motor function six months out, and the estimate was far too imprecise to conclude anything. The Cochrane authors stress that small samples and patchy methodological reporting limit confidence.

This is a student project, not a medical device. It has not been through a clinical trial, it is not FDA cleared, and it is not a substitute for a therapist. It is a way to do more repetitions of an established exercise, on days when getting to a clinic is not possible. Anyone recovering from a stroke should set their programme with a clinician, and this app is something to bring to that conversation — not around it.

Try it, or build it.

The app runs in any modern browser on any phone, tablet or laptop with a camera. The headset files print on any FDM printer with a 200 mm bed, and only one of the eleven parts needs supports.
